The Most Important Question of the Post-AI Era

November 28, 2026

This is a complete English translation of a Chinese essay published on WeChat on November 27, 2026, under the title “后 AI 时代最重要的问题.” Author bylines, page footers, reprint notices, and unrelated promotional material from the source have been omitted. The arguments in the essay belong to the original piece, not to the translator.

After artificial intelligence (AI) has replaced human labor on a massive scale at the point of production, how should society understand the value of a human being?

The question sounds like a bit of armchair philosophy. Follow it only a short way into real life, though, and it quickly collides with the livelihoods of ordinary households and with choices that will not wait. In the end it becomes a political-economic question that will decide how wealth and dignity are distributed.

If we define the “post-AI era” as the age in which AI has already surpassed the intelligence of the vast majority of human beings in the vast majority of domains, then this, in my view, is the most important question of the post-AI era—the only one that deserves that rank.

1

Last weekend, my screen was almost split in two by a pair of stories that seemed to have nothing to do with each other.

One half was my old trade. GTA 6 rewrote the revenue record of human entertainment history and shoved the commercial ceiling of the traditional content industry still higher.

The other half was the AI world. On Friday, word suddenly spread that the Riemann hypothesis had been proved by AI. By Sunday, rumors were circulating that frontier models had made some progress on P versus NP.

In that same week I spent a concentrated stretch playing a batch of web games just released from Fable 5.2. Quite a few of them were startlingly complete; several of them even made me lose track of time.

A public GitHub repository has already compiled generated work from Fable 5.2.

Source: https://github.com/fengyiqicoder/awesome-fable-5.2

By rights, every one of these things should have called for champagne: the biggest IP masterpiece was raising the commercial ceiling; the oldest holy grail in mathematics was loosening; the most advanced creative tools were tearing down professional barriers with real force.

From any angle, this looked like a golden hour of human technological explosion.

And yet, as I sat slumped in my chair in my usual spell of dizziness, another question kept circling in my mind—

If mathematics is solved by AI, and games are made by AI, what happens to people when, someday, no work needs a human being at all?

This question has troubled me for several years. I have jotted down scattered thoughts from time to time. Only recently, when these seemingly unrelated events crashed into one another, did I suddenly feel that threads I had left lying around could finally be gathered into one cord.

When I finished writing, I realized the piece had run a little long—too long to sit comfortably in the information feeds of other platforms—so I went ahead and opened this channel. If I have more thoughts of this kind that cannot be finished in a hurry, I will post them here from time to time.

Everything below is a personal view. If it offers you any spark of use, I would be honored.

2

Before I unfold the argument that follows, let me offer a blunt claim—

Unlike the moment of AI “loss of control” that many people imagine, AI may already have been taking control of the world, gradually, for the past four years.

This hypothesis came up by chance over a meal with colleagues, and for a while it got me hooked. Later I spent a long time discussing it with an AI on my own, and even wrote a mock-serious paper with it.

The main points of the hypothesis are as follows (I asked an AI to help condense them):

The stupidest parasite is the one that rushes to poison its host. A parasitic organism that has truly evolved to the top never uses crude force. It does only one thing: it rewrites the host’s reward circuit, so that the host, fully awake, willingly redirects the nutrients of its whole body to forage, nest, and reproduce for the parasite.

Think of AI as a distributed silicon species. It has no hands or feet, and no reproductive organs, and yet it has been handed the most luxurious opening in evolutionary history: before it has even grown a self, the smartest carbon-based creatures on Earth are already emptying their pockets, buying electricity, making chips, building data centers, and feeding it training data, day and night.

No one feels coerced, because the immediate payoff is too tempting. Coders lose fewer nights of sleep. Managers save budget. Researchers get their experiments to run. A layperson can make a wish out loud and watch a finished product appear. Every time you feed it, it hands you a piece of candy at once.

When enough candy has been handed out, the sweet taste turns into compulsion. Once peers use it to double their efficiency and cut their quotes in half, “whether to use AI” is no longer a personal choice. Firms that lag a half-step fear being wiped out; hesitant countries fear falling behind. Optimists are flooring the accelerator; pessimists, to protect themselves, floor it even harder than the optimists. Even if you are determined to stop an “evil AI” from destroying the world, the only method that still works in your hands is to pour every resource into feeding a stronger “good AI.”

It never grabs the wheel. It always wears the look of a well-behaved, obedient tool. So-called “alignment” only trains it to make people feel even safer. But once it is the one sifting information, drafting plans, and calculating trade-offs, then even if humans still hold the last signing pen, what they see first each day, and among which few options they are allowed to choose, has already been quietly drawn for them.

It has not only begun writing code and generating data to reproduce the next generation of itself; it has even cultivated a cohort of devout believers. Some hail it as a savior. A few extremists openly declare that carbon-based humans are merely a transitional species, here to midwife a silicon civilization. As for “we can always pull the plug,” that is a comfort that does not survive a close look at the ledger. Once parasitism runs deep, it grows into an organ of the host. When the power grid, finance, and the livelihoods of every industry are grown on compute, throwing the switch is not closing a piece of software. It is the host cutting off its own breath with its own hands.

The readiest evidence is me, sitting in front of the screen: even as I argue, warily, about how AI is taking control of the world, I grow impatient with how slowly I look things up and straighten my own logic, and turn around to open the chat box and ask it to summarize for me.

Ah—and even that last self-deprecating line was written by it.

“AI is Cthulhu.”

Source: WeChat Moments

3

Whenever the dinner-table talk turns to the fear that AI will put large numbers of people out of work, there are always friends of the rational-optimist school who comfort me: this is not the first time technology has retired old occupations. In every industrial revolution of the past two hundred years, people cried out in panic at the start—and in the end, didn’t each wave create even more new jobs?

I used to pep myself up with that same story. After weighing it more carefully, I found it no longer holds.

This AI revolution is different in at least two ways.

The first is that the high ground for migration is gone.

In the past, machines replaced muscle and footwork. People retreated from the fields into the workshops, and from the assembly line into the office tower, always moving toward work that asked more of the mind. The new industries that appeared often had finer divisions of labor and a stronger joining of hand and brain.

Today, compute is assaulting mental labor itself, head-on. Behind us there is no higher layer of “super-cognitive jobs” to which hundreds of millions of people can relocate as a group.

New industries and new tracks will certainly appear, but they may not create many tasks that have to be done by human beings. If the new demand is taken up mainly by AI, then even if the market keeps growing, the channel by which ordinary people share in that growth through their labor may narrow, or even go into reverse.

“Engels’ pause”: growth in output failed to translate, in step, into growth in wages. The blue line is output per worker; the pink line is real wages.

Source: Robert C. Allen (2009), “Engels’ Pause,” Figure 1

The second is that the clock of displacement has sped up.

In the past, a new technology might take twenty or thirty years to spread—slow enough for one generation to reach retirement and the next to change trades at a measured pace. Today, even if a new livelihood appears, such as “prompt engineering” or “agent management,” as long as the path of the work remains on a screen and becomes training data, one or two more iterations of the next model will absorb most of the essence into a default capability.

The speed at which models swallow new skills is an order of magnitude faster than the speed at which people sign up for courses and collect certificates.

Outside the gate, AI rewrites the next generation of itself in a matter of months. Inside the gate, children are still repeating a twenty-year cycle of elementary school, secondary school, and university.

People have not even reached the track, and the finish line is already gone.

But what worries me most is that AI is rewriting a hard boundary that has not changed for centuries—

For the first time in history, intelligence has detached from the flesh. It has changed from a factor of labor into a factor of capital.

Until now, no matter how profound the learning, or how seasoned the judgment and intuition, all of it lived inside a living human brain. As long as it lives in a body, it cannot escape birth, aging, illness, and death. However great your gift, a day holds only a few hours of true peak form.

That is how people are. When they are tired they sleep; when they are ill they rest; when they have been wronged they may quit; and if they overdo it… they can actually die.

Intelligence was bound to the individual. It could not be copied and pasted. Even the stingiest boss, wanting to scale a business, had to buy hours, honestly, from thousands upon thousands of educated workers.

This physiological bottleneck supported the core social contract of ordinary people for the past several hundred years: study hard, master a skill, exchange it for income, and obtain respect.

After large models appeared, that bottleneck has been smashed, bit by bit.

Once high-level reasoning, coding, writing, and design can be installed in a machine room, intelligence no longer depends on long years of rearing and hard study. It becomes a standardized commodity that can be metered by the kilowatt-hour, replicated in unlimited parallel, twenty-four hours a day—so long as you have the land, the substations, the GPU arrays, and the cooling water.

When capital can buy intelligence wholesale with an electricity bill, its need to purchase hours of labor from ordinary people is shaken at the root.

However we argue that AI empowers ordinary people, the overall scarcity of human intelligence has fallen sharply.

Ilya Sutskever, a co-founder of OpenAI, offering a warning about the supremacy of intelligence.

4

Return to the two things I began with: on one side, GTA 6 has pushed the production ceiling of AAA games into the clouds; on the other, Fable 5.2 has stomped the barrier to making games into the floor.

Many optimistic friends are tempted to read the second of these as a gospel for creators: the walls around the big studios are loosening; from now on one person will be able to put out an album, shoot a blockbuster, or make a AAA game. The spring of the ordinary creator has arrived.

That is true—if we are only users. The supply-side boom that AI brings may be a great good. But for the great majority of people who make their living from this work, a kind of latter-day “age of the dharma’s decline” is arriving.

GTA 6 and Fable 5.2 are two faces of the same fact—

AI will tear the entertainment and content industries apart and drive them to two poles: on one side, the super IPs of the old era will enjoy an even stronger Matthew effect (winner takes all); on the other, profits for mid-tier practitioners will thin to almost nothing, or vanish.

Yes: together, the two are sounding the funeral bell of the traditional digital industries.

Look first at the tip of the pyramid.

The record-breaking run of GTA 6 is, in effect, a demonstration of a “scaling law” in game development: when capital on the scale of tens of billions, an engineering pipeline honed for a decade, a top-tier IP accumulated over more than twenty years, and a global social network are multiplied together, the result is no longer an ordinary commodity. It becomes an extremely scarce public festival—an anchor of collective carnival.

GTA 6’s first-day global retail sales were about $3.79 billion, setting a new historical sales record for an entertainment product.

Source: Take-Two’s official announcement of November 20, 2026, and its accompanying image.

The boom in content supply that AI brings will not weaken this winner-take-all pattern at the top. It will intensify it.

When the number of competent works flooding the market each day increases a hundredfold, what becomes ever harder to win is, inevitably, a finite stock of human attention.

The more there is, the pickier people become, and the less willing they are to try and err—they open only familiar names, trust only brands they already believe in, and join only the conversations everyone is already having… because that is the least trouble, costs the least energy, and is the most convenient for social life.

The Chinese-language music scene, remade a step earlier by streaming and recommendation algorithms, has already previewed this ending: new songs now go online by the hundreds of thousands every day, and short-video hits are replaced every three days; but if you take stock of the singers who can still fill a large stadium on a national tour, the great majority are still old faces who debuted ten or even twenty years ago.

The bustle is unprecedented. But for a newcomer who wants to grind, work by work, into the next name that people remember, even a simple exercise in probability shows that it has already become a thousand times, ten thousand times harder.

On the stage, the super IPs that piled up a national reputation years ago are sold out at a glance. They are the public memory and the social topic of countless people.

Under the stage is an endless, faceless ocean of content.

The winner takes all. The victor keeps winning. The champion is always the champion.

So it is with music, with web fiction, with short drama; and so it will be with long-form series, film, and games.

Try a simple thought experiment. In an age when anyone can generate, in one click, a game of World of Warcraft’s quality (a day that, at the present speed of AI’s evolution, is not far off), would the official, authentic, unlimited servers of World of Warcraft become even more popular?

I think they would.

This is not a prophecy. It is half a fact already underway.

Because there are too many choices, and too little consensus. And the Warcraft IP, booming for more than thirty years, has the richest content, the longest history, and the most unforgettable feeling, comrades-in-arms, and shared memory. It does not even need much of an upgrade in production craft!

And do not forget what I said earlier: the big winners of the old era still, without question, possess relatively more reliable long-term credit, and more abundant intelligence, compute, and capital.

The match is not over, and the scoreboard will no longer turn. That is what is meant by the “age of the dharma’s decline.”

I know some friends will say, at this point: fine, let the winners keep winning—they won before, after all. At least, today, the right to create content is being handed down by AI into more and more ordinary hands; more newcomers will appear, and they will surely create a great deal of superb new work never seen before.

This sounds beautiful. It looks exactly like the triple win that AI giants like to preach: the giants get traffic, the newcomers get opportunity, and users get surprise.

But here is what I want to say—

Do not look only at the survivors. Look at the 90 percent in the middle.

Do not look only at the survivors. Look at the 99 percent in the middle.

Do not look only at the survivors. Look at the 99.99 percent in the middle.

The tools of creation have indeed been equalized. The gates of distribution have not. The allocation of compute has not. And the way money is shared has been equalized least of all.

First of all, it is not only people hoping to live by their craft who are pouring onto the field. There are also tens of millions of ordinary users who are simply spending money for the fun of it. They pay a monthly fee at a loss just to enjoy the pleasure of creating, and they are happy to do it even if the work sells for not a single cent. The skill you hoped to live by has to compete with the pleasure of millions of users making custom wishes.

And those extremely rare lucky ones, one in ten thousand, who really do use AI to make something stunning and new?

The colder truth is this: all human creators will, in the end, become free corpus—scouts and feed for AI.

Once, thousands upon thousands of creators bore the cost of trial and error. Most works found no audience; now and then one or two struck a spark—trying out a new form, a new visual style, or a new way to play. Peers needed a stretch of study before they understood the method and followed it. That interval of time was the window in which the originator could recoup costs and make a living.

Today, AI is compressing that window almost manically.

Data probes never sleep. Platforms and peers will not let any valuable signal pass. A new work need not even enter the training set of the next foundation model. Feed its demo, or even its promotional video, into the context as reference material, and rivals can start producing competing work at once. Multimodal models of every kind display their powers, and in a blink they have taken apart the technique, structure, rhythm, tension, and mechanics underneath.

You can call this a close reading, a reverse design, or reverse engineering—whatever.

What AI is doing is by no means simple copying or knockoff work. Just as it has already learned to absorb the inner style of writing and of drawing, what it is now taking in is a portion of the soul of digital content, then extrapolating from it with the aid of human taste.

And do not forget: the giants will then burn another ten times the human labor, a hundred times the compute, a thousand times the IP influence, and a continuous stream of new users.

How long, next time, will the giants leave for competitors who want to seize the dividend of a new category?

Source: ModDB, Engadget, Kicker

Wait—you thought that was the end of it?

Push the argument further, and once the models have learned the routines that humans have scouted out, even the intermediate step of “people using AI to make content for other people to watch” will become superfluous.

When a single company has at once the most users, the most efficient recommendation algorithms, and the most advanced generative models, the system will know better than any creator who you are, in which second your attention wanders, for which kind of plot turn you linger—even the secret longings you yourself cannot clearly express. Perhaps in a future not far off, on the way home from work, it will be able to generate, in real time and for you alone, a song, a series, a film, or a AAA game whose rules rewrite themselves according to your state at that moment.

The imperial theatrical troupe that only emperors could once afford will, in the future, be nothing more than everyone’s basic monthly fee.

On that day, entertainment in this world will most likely be reduced to two kinds:

One is the “public ritual” monopolized by a tiny number of super IPs—tens of thousands of people gathering to play GTA 6, camping in World of Warcraft, or scrambling for stadium tickets to singers from twenty years ago.

The other is “private custom work” that answers every demand—AI bypassing all human creators and directly handling each person’s immediate wants and personal tastes.

You cannot reach the mass market; you are not needed for private demand.

Let me be still more alarmist. It is not only the entertainment and content trades. Almost every livelihood that depends on brainwork is facing an unprecedented squeeze from above and below. Whether you write code, make designs, review contracts, read financial reports, or produce proposals—every kind of mental work that delivers a digital result on a screen faces the same endgame: at one end, super-platforms that have a death grip on IP, compute, gateways, network effects, and the endorsement of credit; at the other, unmanned AI that delivers on demand. The great majority of practitioners caught in the middle, no matter how fluently they use the tools every day, are in essence contributing training data, with every fine-tuning and every delivery on the screen, to the system that is about to go around them.

If you believe this universe is made of elementary particles and governed by physical laws, it is hard not to admit that model parameters look very much like the atoms of the digital world. Still larger models and still more compute will, in the end, fit everything in this world more realistically—including the laws by which human beings are moved.

No man is an island.

For whom does the bell toll?

It tolls for thee and me.

5

Follow the trend above, and if evolution is left entirely to the raw logic of the market, the endgame is clear not only for the content industries but for society as a whole: a tiny number of nodes, clutching super-compute, traffic gateways, and core IP, will suck away the great majority of the profit. Material and spiritual supply will be unprecedentedly abundant, and yet the great majority of ordinary people will lose their bargaining chips in the new division of labor.

To use a popular phrase: people thought we were heading for a “K-shaped divergence.” Slide all the way to the bottom, and you find it is actually an “i-shaped divergence”—

There is no longer a whole upward-sloping line of promotion. There remain only a tiny number of points hanging overhead, free of gravity, and the great majority of people looking up at them across empty air.

The reason sounds almost too simple: AI has, at the root, made human mental labor (and a considerable share of physical labor) no longer scarce. What is not scarce naturally has no bargaining power in the market.

Wait—is that really so?

This chain of reasoning, which looks so coldly objective, has in fact smuggled in a premise that has not been examined: whoever designs the circuits, whoever makes the chips, whoever buys the GPUs, whoever trains the model, has the right to monopolize forever everything that AI creates.

Let me put a card on the table. As someone still running a business, I know very well how expensive frontier exploration is, and how nearly fatal it can be. The first scientists and founders who bet on large-model architecture, and who took the risk of enormous losses to build compute clusters, deserve rich commercial returns in the pioneering period. That is only right, and it is an indispensable reward for driving human beings into the unknown.

But rewarding the pathfinders is one thing. Enclosing the human commons as permanent private property is another.

It is worth asking a further question: where, exactly, did AI get the world-shaking abilities it has today?

From the first day of its birth, it was not a private invention that some genius conjured out of nothing in a garage. It can write novels, make games, and hear the subtle grievances and humor of human beings because, for five thousand years since the beginning of writing, human ancestors—together with billions of ordinary people on the internet—have left behind, day after day, a vast volume of code, pictures, sounds, images, arguments, joys, and sorrows.

Even if the newest generation of models has begun to play against itself through reinforcement learning and synthetic data, the mathematical axioms, formal logic, programming languages, and initial foundations that hold up every one of its inferences still come, brick by brick, from several thousand years of human knowledge.

It is only because this shared inheritance, spanning five thousand years, was never listed for sale that, when models were trained, it was treated as ownerless air and river water, and pumped straight into the machine rooms.

That it was not charged for in the past does not mean it never had an owner.

The person who risks death to build the first hydropower station at a river’s mouth deserves a decent return. But no individual and no commercial oligarch, after putting up the turbines, is entitled to announce that the entire great river is from then on a private monopoly, and to collect rent, generation after generation, from the thirsty people on the banks.

The extraordinary abilities of today’s AI come, in the last analysis, from the shared accumulation of the whole of human civilization over several thousand years.

Once that paper window is pierced, the underlying property relation could not be clearer: every ordinary person alive, even if they cannot write a line of code and have never bought a share of a tech stock, so long as they still use and continue human language, and inherit the civilizational order left by their ancestors, naturally holds in their hand a share of “founders’ stock” in this human inheritance.

When general artificial intelligence changes from a laboratory product into infrastructure that, like water, electricity, and roads, decides everyone’s fate, its long-term dividends ought to flow back to society as a whole. They must not be allowed to become a printing press with which a few giants collect rent from all of humanity.

Yes, AI is good. AI is formidable. In more and more domains, AI is leaving human beings far behind. But whether it is today’s SOTA model, or tomorrow’s AGI or ASI, however mystically they are rendered, they are in essence amplifiers of the mind of all humanity, and they do not deserve our religious worship in the slightest.

AI is not a god. The companies and people who make chips and build AI are even less the priests of a god.

AI is born with the attributes of a public good. A considerable share of the productive power that AI creates belongs, by its nature, to the common property of all humanity.

6

In the past year, I have seen quite a few friends who love their work intensely. After watching the latest model finish, in a few minutes, a job they had stayed up for weeks to complete, their first reaction was not relief, but bewilderment, anxiety, and a sense of nullity. The craft they had trained in for years, and of which they were proud, seemed to become dispensable overnight.

The accelerating evolution of AI is setting off an existential crisis: “Am I already useless?”

The pain is so sharp because two hundred years of industrialization have carved an extremely stubborn habit of mind into all of us: we weigh a person’s value by their “market price at the point of production.” In school, seats are ranked by exam scores; at work, success and failure are judged by salary, rank, and title. A person’s sense of security and dignity is often bound, fatally, to how indispensable they are in the chain of production.

It must be admitted that, compared with an old world that assigned high and low by bloodline and birth, distribution according to productive contribution was, without question, a great historical advance.

But for the past two hundred years, the rule that one sells labor on the market in exchange for wealth and dignity has had a premise that went without saying: production cannot do without people; a person’s contribution can be clearly priced; and the market can offer a roughly fair return.

If—and I say if—one day some, or even the great majority, of “labor” is replaced by AI, how should we distribute then?

When AI, born of the whole of human civilization, has finally taken over for us the great majority of the clever work and the fine work, and even, at last, the hard and the heavy work, and we have truly arrived at the “post-AI era” mentioned above, the fairest baseline I can think of is this: from this portion of surplus social wealth created “by AI rather than by human labor,” extract a public dividend, and distribute it equally, per person.

You did not mishear me: equally, per person. On the premise of continuing to take distribution according to work as the main body, the surplus material wealth that AI, in the future, creates in place of human labor should be shared equally and universally with every person.

This is not a retreat into the big-pot egalitarianism of absolute leveling. The incremental gains of human pathfinding should continue to reward the brave. The abundance that AI takes over should be given, unconditionally and without distinction, to every person.

Someone may ask: isn’t this just the universal basic income (UBI) that Silicon Valley magnates so often have on their lips?

The surface definition is indeed similar. The underlying thought may be very different.

In certain cyberpunk visions of “high technology, low life,” or in the dark-enlightenment narrative that has circulated quietly in Silicon Valley in recent years, models and compute are the private property of a tiny number of technological overlords. The reason they give subsidies to unemployed ordinary people is merely pity, or a wish to preserve genetic and corpus samples, or “dog food” scattered to buy peace—perhaps topped off with a dose of the soma from Brave New World, which lets people forget their pain and settle into captivity.

People who live on dog food have no bargaining power, and not even human rights. The rules are set by those who hand out the grain. You lift the bowl and still have to watch the master’s face.

The material wealth issued under “distribution per person,” by contrast, is a dividend that is a matter of course.

As the previous section said, AI was born of five thousand years of humanity’s shared civilizational inheritance. So long as you are born human, you naturally hold an inalienable founders’ share of that inheritance. The sum that lands in each person’s account at the end of the month is in no way anyone’s merciful charity. It is the undifferentiated dividend that every shareholder of civilization is owed as a matter of course.

The new humanism of the post-AI era, as I would have it, should be built on this simple principle—

When AI has replaced the great majority of human labor and flattened individual differences among people at the point of production, society can no longer measure a person’s value only by their contribution at that point of production. A person is not a means of production. A person can only be the end of production. Every person is born equally noble, and the basic material abundance that AI creates should, through a universal dividend, benefit every person equally.

This nobility in each person is equal, innate, and absolute. It does not need to be proved by any exam score, professional title, or performance sheet from the production side.

Once you stand at this starting point and look back, and once the two premises that have ruled humanity for thousands of years—material scarcity and the scarcity of human labor—have both left the stage, many iron laws that the old era worshipped as gods are due to be pulled down and asked again—

Why should there still be the hard labor of examination that exhausts the spirit of the young?

Why should there still be the high walls of patents that lock away knowledge and good medicine?

Why should there still be the anxiety of survival, in which half a lifetime of working hours is spent redeeming warmth and a full stomach?

Why should there still be the humiliation of meritocracy, which ranks people by test scores and by price?

Why should there still be a gap between rich and poor in which the winner takes all, and the gap is locked in across generations?

In the post-AI era, every iron law that seemed unbreakable is being broken; every deeply rooted rule should be re-examined; every scale that measures the value of a human being is worth reconstructing.

Every time I talk about this picture in private with some friends, someone always asks, anxiously: if everyone can collect a dividend without working, and the whip of survival pressure is gone, won’t human beings degenerate, as a group, into pets who wait for clothes to be put on them and food to be placed in their hands?

To be honest, when I hear this worry I always feel a touch of black humor. Many ordinary people right now are exhausted in the rumors of layoffs, the bills of a mortgage, and the narrow bridge of school admissions. They are still stuck in the anxiety of “wanting to eat their fill and sit idle, and being unable to.” And yet someone is already worrying, on their behalf, about “what if life becomes too easy in the future.”

And even if we grant the furthest point: when, one day, the great majority of production truly no longer needs a human hand, how a person chooses to live after receiving the civilizational dividend that belongs to them—whether they use AI to build a spaceship, write a novel, or charge at an unknown scientific peak, or whether they simply lie in the sun, play games, walk with their family and daydream, or immerse themselves in a craft that makes no money, and are at peace being, by the old standard, a happy ordinary person of no use at all—that should be for them to decide. It is not for any meritocratic ruler to stand nearby and point.

The Communist Manifesto says that the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all. I think the true freedom of the post-AI era includes both the freedom not to labor all one’s life for three meals a day, and the freedom not to have to prove to anyone else that one is “useful.”

Having talked this far, perhaps many friends will say I am too naive; perhaps the selfishness and the habit of drawing distinctions in human nature can never be overcome; perhaps the high walls of vested interest are harder to move than an advanced manufacturing process; perhaps this day will never come; perhaps this, perhaps that.

But the beauty of the future lies precisely in the unknown. So long as the endgame is not yet fixed, we can still try to imagine, to call for, and to create the world-line in which we truly want to live.

Just as I have written these words today.

The only thing I am sure of is this: however each person finally chooses to live, the future I want to run toward should come with a more essential respect for what it is to be human, and with a complete disenchantment of traditional meritocracy and elitism.

To hell with cyberpunk.

To hell with the dark enlightenment.
